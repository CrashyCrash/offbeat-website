LANDR vs Splice: a sample library against a finishing service

They overlap just enough to be compared, and not enough to be substitutes. Splice is where a track starts; LANDR is where it gets mastered, distributed and paid for. Here is what each costs in 2026, and the one limitation in LANDR's cheap tier that catches people at release time.

By Offbeat Editorial TeamReviewed October 20268 min read
Compare the costHow we research

Half the comparison pages about these two get stuck because they treat "music production platform" as one category. It is not. Splice sells raw material: loops, one-shots, presets, MIDI, and rent-to-own instruments. LANDR sells the last mile: a master, a release to 150+ stores, and a bundle of plugins and sample credits thrown in so the subscription looks like a studio.

Both now include samples, which is why people compare them. The overlap is real but shallow: LANDR's sample allowance is a few thousand credits a year, and Splice's mastering tool is a plugin bundle, not a service. Decide by which end of the process is currently costing you time.

The one catch in each

Splice: credits reward browsing, not finishing

One hundred credits a month sounds generous until you are three packs deep at 1 a.m. and have downloaded 40 kick drums you will never open. The credit model is designed to make collecting feel like progress. The producers who get value from Splice treat it as a library card: find the sound, download it, close the tab, go back to the arrangement. Credits roll over while you are subscribed, which is helpful — and they lapse about a month after you cancel, which is not.

LANDR: the cheap tier's masters are MP3

This is the detail worth knowing before you release something. The entry tier's unlimited mastering is MP3-only, with WAV masters capped on the lower plans and unlimited only higher up. Stores and distributors want a WAV or FLAC for release, so an MP3 master is for demos, DJ edits and referencing — not the file you send to Spotify. If you are releasing, price the tier that gives you WAVs, or pay per master at roughly $10 a track.

Neither catch is a scam. Both are the ordinary shape of subscription pricing: the entry price buys access, the useful thing sits one tier up.

Where each one sits in an actual release

A track from a DJ/producer goes through five stages, and the two services cover different ones:

StageWhat it needsWho covers it
1. Idea and sound designLoops, one-shots, presets, a fast way to auditionSplice — its library and the Bridge plugin are the reason people subscribe
2. Arrangement and mixA DAW you know, monitoring you trustNeither. This is your DAW and your room.
3. MasterA WAV that is loud enough and not crushedLANDR, or a human engineer at roughly $50–150 a track
4. DistributionDelivery to Spotify, Apple, Beatport-adjacent storesLANDR (or DistroKid / TuneCore — see our comparison)
5. Selling to DJsA store page, a download, a priceBandcamp — see SoundCloud vs Bandcamp

That table is the answer most people actually need. If stage one is your bottleneck, buy Splice. If stage three or four is, buy LANDR. If both are, that is a sign you have not finished a track recently enough to know which one hurts.

Verdict

Choose Splice if

Your bottleneck is sounds

You are writing regularly, you want a deep library with a licence that covers releases, and you already have a way to put music out. The credit model rewards discipline: set a monthly download budget and stop when it is spent. Rent-to-own on plugins is genuinely useful if you would otherwise buy them outright.

Choose LANDR if

Your bottleneck is finishing

You have tracks that sound close but sit quiet next to commercial releases, and no plan for getting them onto stores. Pick the tier that includes WAV masters — the entry tier's MP3-only allowance is the detail that turns a "cheap" plan into a wasted year.

If you are a DJ who makes the occasional edit or bootleg for your own sets, neither is the right first purchase: you need a DAW you already have and a mastering engineer for the one track a year you actually release. Buy the subscription when the limitation starts costing you takes, not before.

Before you buy: five checks

Audio subscriptions are contracts and licences, not purchases. These are the terms that matter:

Does the licence cover commercial releasesRoyalty-free should mean you can monetise the track, not just upload it.
Which master formats your tier includesMP3 is a demo; WAV or FLAC is a release. Check before you plan a release schedule.
What survives cancellationDownloads, credits, hosted releases and plugin licences behave differently. Write down which is which.
Whether the subscription is annual-billedThe monthly price usually assumes a year. Compare the annual total to per-track purchases.
Whether you can pauseA plan you can pause for two months is worth more than one you have to cancel and rejoin.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is LANDR or Splice better value?

They price different jobs. Splice costs $12.99 a month for 100 download credits from a multi-million sample library; LANDR Studio starts around $8.25 a month on annual billing and bundles limited-format mastering, unlimited distribution and a smaller sample allowance. If you already have a distributor, Splice is the useful purchase; if you have finished tracks and no release path, LANDR covers more ground.

Do I need both?

Many producers end up with both, and it is the plan we would suggest if you release regularly: Splice for sounds and instruments during writing, LANDR (or a paid mastering engineer plus a cheap distributor) for the finish. The alternative is to buy sample packs outright and pay per master, which costs less if you release two tracks a year.

What is the catch with LANDR's cheap mastering tier?

The unlimited masters on the entry tier are MP3. WAV masters are capped on the lower tiers and unlimited only higher up. Since distributors and stores expect a WAV or FLAC master for release, an MP3-only allowance is for demos and DJ edits, not for the version you distribute.

Can Splice distribute my music?

No — Splice is a sample, preset and plugin subscription with rent-to-own instrument options. Distribution is a separate job, handled by LANDR, DistroKid, TuneCore, Amuse and similar services. The one exception worth knowing is that Splice integrates with DAWs and plugins, not with stores.

Are Splice samples really cleared for release?

Splice's licence lets you use downloaded samples in commercial releases, including releases you monetise, and downloads stay yours after you cancel. The catch is that this covers the sample as supplied: if you are recreating a specific record, that is a different legal question. Credits remaining at cancellation expire about 28 days later.

Which is better for a beginner producer?

Splice, at first. Beginners need sounds and momentum more than a mastering chain, and 100 credits a month is enough to finish tracks. Move to LANDR, or per-track mastering, when you have something worth releasing and the mix is already translating on three different systems.
