Buying your first controller, in the right order

The controller is rarely the thing holding a new DJ back, and it is almost always the first thing they buy. Here is the order that actually works: what to own, what to skip, what the money buys at each price, and how to check a deck fits your software before you pay for it.

By Offbeat Editorial TeamUpdated October 20269 min read
Plan the whole rigSee the beginner picks

The first controller is where most beginners lose money, and not because they buy a bad one. They buy the right deck at the wrong moment, with nothing left to plug into it, or they spend three months trying to fix a technique problem by replacing hardware. I have watched this happen often enough that the sequence is predictable — so this page is that sequence, not another ranking.

If you already know your budget and your software, jump to where each price band lands. If you are at the very start, read the next two sections; they save the most money.

Start with what you already have

You do not need to spend anything to find out whether you enjoy this. A laptop, a pair of ordinary headphones and a free copy of rekordbox, Serato DJ Lite, VirtualDJ or djay is enough to learn the parts of DJing that are not physical: counting phrases, hearing where a track's intro ends, working out which two records belong together. That is most of the job. The controller adds two hands' worth of muscle memory on top of it.

What a laptop alone will not teach you is the thing this site spends the most words on: beatmatching by ear. A keyboard shortcut for sync will always be there, and a real pitch fader under your fingers is a different skill with a different feedback loop. If you can, borrow a controller for a week before buying one — a friend's DDJ-400 and an evening of unquantised mixing will tell you more than any review.

What the money actually buys

Controller prices look like a ladder of quality. In practice they are a ladder of specific features, and only four of them matter in the first year:

FeatureWhy it matters earlyWhen you can ignore it
A pitch fader with real travelBeatmatching by ear needs fine, repeatable adjustment. Short plastic sliders make small corrections impossible, so you learn to rely on sync.Never, if you intend to learn manual mixing.
Headphone cue with a cue/mix knobYou cannot mix what you cannot hear privately. Split-cue on a phone is a workaround, not a workflow.Never. Every setup needs this.
Jog wheels with any weight to themLight jogs move when you breathe on them; you spend the evening correcting instead of mixing.If you only ever play cue-point-to-cue-point with sync.
Full-size output jacksRCA-only outputs mean an adapter for every PA you meet. One XLR or TRS pair saves years of adapters.When you are practising into a hi-fi in one room.

Everything else on the spec sheet — pad count, on-board effects, screens, RGB — is genuinely fun and genuinely not what makes a first year go well. The feature list that decides whether you keep practising is short, which is why a used mid-range deck often beats a new entry-level one at the same money.

The order to spend money in

Spending in this order means every purchase makes the next one useful, and no purchase sits in a box waiting for something else:

  1. Headphones. Closed-back, replaceable pads, at least one detachable cable. Around $99–$199 buys a pair that survives a few years. This is the only purchase on the list you will still be using with your second controller.
  2. The controller. Bought with the software licence you intend to stay on. One deck, two channels, four pads and a real pitch fader is plenty.
  3. Cables. One RCA-to-anything, one 3.5 mm-to-6.35 mm headphone adapter, one spare USB cable of the right length. Nobody budgets for these and everybody needs them.
  4. Speakers or a room. Practise on what you own until the neighbours complain. Studio monitors are for producing, not for DJing; a second-hand pair of powered PA speakers with 5-inch drivers is more useful at home.
  5. The laptop, if yours is struggling. Only replace it when audio dropouts actually happen, and then buy for CPU and a decent SSD, not for a graphics card.

Two things belong on the later list: a separate mixer (your controller has one) and a second controller (no matter how the internet makes it feel).

The upgrade trap

Three problems get blamed on hardware more than any others. None of them are solved by a more expensive controller:

  • "My mixes drift." That is tempo-matching practice, not jog quality. The trainer and the beatmatching guide fix this; a $1,700 deck does not.
  • "My transitions sound abrupt." Usually phrasing — bringing a track in on the wrong bar — or EQ, not the pads. Ten minutes with a phrase-counting exercise changes more than an upgrade.
  • "I cannot find my cue points." That is preparation: hot cues set before the set, labelled, in the order you will need them. Software does this on any controller.

The honest upgrade trigger is narrower. Buy the next thing when a specific thing you want to do becomes impossible: you need a third and fourth channel, you are playing rooms that only accept XLR, or you are carrying gear to gigs and the plastic deck is flexing. Otherwise the money is better spent on music.

New, refurbished or used

Refurbished direct from the manufacturer is the best-kept secret in this hobby: the same warranty terms as new, usually 15–25% off, and a returns process. Used is fine when you can test it, and the test takes ten minutes:

  • Every fader and knob through its full travel, slowly, listening for crackle or a dead zone at one end.
  • Both headphone outputs, both main outputs, one at a time.
  • Every pad, every button, every jog — and the touch strip on the jog if it has one.
  • A real track playing out of both channels while you move the crossfader.
  • The bundled software licence: is it transferred, an unlock, or a trial that has already expired?

Skip any listing that will not show the unit powered on. "Boxed, barely used, no charger" is the same as "returns are your problem".

Four checks before you pay

  1. Software edition. Lite, LE, Pro or full? A rekordbox unlock is worth more than a slightly better jog wheel; a Serato DJ Lite licence will not record your mix without an upgrade.
  2. Operating system. Check the manufacturer's support page for your exact macOS or Windows version, not the marketing page. Audio drivers are where old laptops fall over.
  3. Outputs. Confirm what is in the box: RCA, TRS, XLR, and whether a power supply is included or USB-bus powered.
  4. Size. Measure the desk. A four-channel deck is 60 cm wide before you add speakers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I buy first: a controller or headphones?

Headphones, if you already have something to play on. Closed-back headphones are how you cue the next track without the room hearing it, and every controller needs a pair. A first controller with no headphones is a setup you cannot mix on.

Do I need a laptop to use a DJ controller?

Usually yes — most controllers are midi surfaces for software running on a laptop or a phone. Standalone systems run without one and cost considerably more. There is no wrong answer, but the laptop is part of the budget on almost every beginner setup, including the cheap ones.

Is the DDJ-FLX4 still the default first controller?

For most people starting in late 2026, yes: it is around $329, runs rekordbox and Serato DJ Lite, and has a long enough pitch fader to learn to beatmatch by ear. If your budget is closer to $150 or you want a standalone unit, the answer changes — the bands table below explains where the line sits.

Is it safe to buy a used controller?

Sometimes. Test the faders through their full travel, both headphone outputs, every pad, and a real track playing out of both channels before money changes hands. Ask whether the bundled software licence transfers — on some controllers it does not, and you would be buying a $200 midi keyboard with jog wheels.

How do I know a controller works with my software?

Check the manufacturer's current support page for your exact model and operating system, then confirm which software edition is included (Lite, LE or full) and whether it is an unlock or a time-limited trial. Our compatibility matrix lists the pairings we have confirmed.

What can I skip on a first setup?

A separate mixer, a second controller, a flight case, and anything that only matters on a stage. Buy the cheapest thing that lets you practise for an hour without fighting the gear, then upgrade once a specific limitation starts costing you takes.
