How to Beatmatch Manually Without Sync (Step-by-Step Guide)

Master manual beatmatching on DJ controllers and turntables — pitch adjustment, tempo matching, and timing correction without pressing Sync.

✍️ By Offbeat Editorial Team📅 Updated June 2026⏱️ 5 min read

Perfect beatmatching is the skill that separates working DJs from bedroom hobbyists. It's not hard — but it does take deliberate practice over several weeks. Here's the method that builds the skill step by step.

Why Learn Manual Beatmatching?

Sync works. But relying on Sync creates a gap in your ear training that will surface at the worst moment — when Sync fails, when BPM detection is wrong on an unusual track, or when you switch to CDJs without a grid-corrected library. DJs who can beatmatch manually can recover from any error. DJs who can't are stuck. It's also the whole skillset on vinyl, where turntable DJing has no sync to fall back on.

Step 1: Match BPMs with the Pitch Fader

Play Track 1 in your master channel. Load Track 2 in your cue channel. Listen to Track 2 in your headphones — NOT through the speakers. Start Track 2 and use the pitch fader to slowly adjust its BPM until the tempo feels equal to Track 1. Most controllers show BPM in software — use that as a starting reference, then trust your ears over the numbers. If the two tracks are within a few BPM of each other, narrow the pitch fader range in your software (for example ±8 instead of ±16) — the finer control is worth it.

Step 2: Find the Downbeat

Most dance music has a 4/4 time signature with a strong kick drum on beat 1. Listen for it. Mark your cue point at beat 1 of the next phrase (usually every 16 or 32 bars). This is your drop-in point.

Step 3: Tap in the Track

Stop Track 2. On beat 1 of a phrase in Track 1, restart Track 2 from your cue point. Listen in headphones to both tracks simultaneously. At this stage they're probably slightly off — that's normal. In split-cue mode (left ear on master, right ear on cue), a well-timed entry sounds like a single unified kick; two separate kick sounds means you're out of phase.

Step 4: Nudge to Correct Phase

If Track 2 is ahead of Track 1 (its downbeats land before Track 1's), lightly push the jog wheel forward to slow it down. If Track 2 is behind, pull the jog wheel back to speed it up. Make small corrections — never big jolts. The tracks will lock into phase after a few nudges. If the kicks keep drifting even though the BPMs already match, the fix is phase, not tempo — use nudges, not the pitch fader, for that.

Step 5: Blend and Crossfade

Confirm the alignment in cue — a single unified kick on every beat — for 16 to 32 bars before bringing the incoming track in. Once the beats are locked, bring up Track 2's EQ (boost its bass, cut Track 1's bass to avoid frequency clash), bring up the volume fader, and crossfade smoothly. The whole blend should take 16–32 bars.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to learn beatmatching?

It varies a lot from person to person. With 30 minutes of focused daily practice, many people can make reliable 4/4 mixes without sync after a few weeks, and effortless, consistent mixing keeps building from there. The pace mainly depends on how much you practice and how carefully you listen.

Should I turn off Sync when practicing?

Yes. Turn off Sync and Quantize while learning. Re-enable them for live performance after you have the manual skill.

Is beatmatching different on CDJs vs a controller?

The fundamental technique is identical. CDJ jog wheels are larger which makes nudging easier, but the ear training and pitch fader method are the same.
