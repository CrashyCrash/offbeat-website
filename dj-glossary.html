The DJ glossary

Every term you will hear in a booth, a forum or a shop — defined in plain language, with the guide that explains the ones that matter. 73 entries, no jargon explaining jargon.

Offbeat EditorialReviewed October 2026Reference
Look something upStart DJing

Note: This page has no affiliate links. Where a term matters, it links to the guide that covers it properly.

DJ vocabulary is a mix of technical terms, trade slang and brand names that became generic. Some of it is useful shorthand; some of it exists to make buying gear feel more complicated than it is. This glossary keeps the first and translates the second.

If you are new, start with beatmatch, phrase and cue. Those three explain most of what a DJ actually does.

A–Z DJ Glossary

Booth monitor
A speaker in the DJ booth that plays the master mix back at the DJ, so you can hear what the room hears without relying on the main PA. Also called a booth speaker or booth out. Gear and setup
Beatgrid
The mapping of beats inside a software track file. Sync, quantise and looping all rely on it being correct. A wrong grid makes a good record unmixable. Software
Beatjump
Moving the playhead of a track forward or backward by a set number of beats, usually 1, 4 or 8. Used to fix a misaligned entry without touching the pitch. Mixing technique
Beatmatch
Aligning two tracks so their beats land together — first the tempo, then the phase. The core manual skill behind every blend. Mixing technique
BPM
Beats per minute. The tempo, usually 120–130 for house, 170+ for drum & bass. Half and double time describe the same grid at different speeds. Mixing technique
Breakdown
The section of a track where the drums drop out or thin, leaving melody or vocal. A useful place to bring a new track in. Mixing technique
Camelot wheel
A circular key notation (1A–12B) that shows which musical keys mix well together. Harmonic mixing is easier with it than with note names. Mixing technique
Cartridge
The removable assembly holding the stylus on a turntable, including the headshell in most DJ designs. Changing it changes the sound and the tracking. Gear and setup
CDJ
Pioneer's club-standard media player. Plays from USB, SD or link. Not a CD player in practice — most DJs never put a disc in one. Gear and setup
Channel fader
The volume control for one channel on a mixer or controller, as opposed to the crossfader which blends two. Gear and setup
Clamp force
How hard headphone cups press against your head. High clamp means better isolation and a shorter comfortable session. Gear and setup
Closed-back
Headphone cups that seal against the ear and block outside sound. The only sensible choice for cueing over a loud PA. Gear and setup
Controller
A single unit combining decks, mixer and audio interface, controlled by software on a laptop, phone or tablet. Gear and setup
Cue
Two meanings: the act of previewing a track in headphones before the room hears it, and a marked point in a track you can jump back to. Context decides. Mixing technique
Cue point
A saved position in a track, usually on a hot cue pad. Hot cues survive a track reload; a temporary cue does not. Software
Crossfader
The horizontal fader that blends channel A into channel B. Essential for scratching; often left centred by club DJs who mix with channel faders. Gear and setup
Cutting
A scratch technique where the crossfader chops the sound on and off in time with the record. Baby scratch, chirp and transform are all cuts. Mixing technique
DVS
Digital Vinyl System. Special control records send a timecode signal to software, so you can scratch digital files with real turntables. Software
Dead cat
A furry windshield for a microphone. Matters if you record outdoors; irrelevant in a booth. Gear and setup
Direct drive
A turntable motor connected straight to the platter, with high torque and immediate start. The standard for DJ use; belt drive is not. Gear and setup
Downbeat
Beat 1 of a bar — the strongest beat in most dance music. Finding it is the second half of beatmatching. Mixing technique
Drop
The moment a track's main section arrives after a build. The most reliable place to complete a blend. Mixing technique
EQ
Equaliser. Three-band EQ (low, mid, high) on each channel lets you swap basslines cleanly during a blend. Mixing technique
Engine DJ
Denon's standalone ecosystem: library software plus the operating system inside Prime players. Software
Fader curve
How a fader responds to movement — sharp for cutting, gradual for blending. Most mixers let you switch it. Gear and setup
Filter
A tone control that sweeps frequencies out rather than boosting them. The most-used effect in modern DJing. Mixing technique
Gain / trim
The input level control at the top of each channel. Set it so the meter peaks in the amber, not the red. Mixing technique
Ground loop
The hum you get when two pieces of gear are earthed through different paths. A €20 isolator usually fixes it. Gear and setup
Harmonic mixing
Mixing in compatible musical keys so melodies do not clash. The Camelot wheel is the usual shorthand. Mixing technique
Headroom
The space between your loudest signal and distortion. Leave some, or your set will sound flat and clipped. Mixing technique
Hot cue
A pad-assigned jump point in a track, stored with the file. Used for drops, vocals and instant reloads. Software
Jog wheel
The platter on a controller used to nudge, scratch or search within a track. Size and weight are the main feel differences between controllers. Gear and setup
Key lock / master tempo
Holds a track's pitch while you change its tempo. Essential for harmonic mixing; it costs a little CPU. Software
Latency
The delay between an action and the sound. Over about 20 ms it becomes noticeable when cueing; over about 40 ms it is unworkable for scratching. Software
Limiter
A fast compressor that stops peaks from clipping. Good for protecting a PA; bad as a substitute for gain staging. Mixing technique
Loop
A repeated section of a track, set by beat length. Loops are how you buy time mid-mix. Mixing technique
Master output
The final mix sent to the room. What the audience hears, as opposed to the cue bus in your headphones. Gear and setup
MIDI
A control protocol, not audio. Controllers send MIDI or HID messages; the software makes the sound. Software
Mixer
The unit that combines channels, applies EQ and effects and sends the result to the PA. Separate mixers exist for vinyl and CDJ setups. Gear and setup
Monitor
Either a booth speaker or a studio speaker. Context decides which. Gear and setup
Needle
Informal name for the stylus. Also what you should not drop on a record. Gear and setup
Normal / thru
How a mixer routes an unused channel. Thru passes the signal straight out; normal sums it into the mix. Gear and setup
Open format
A set that moves across genres — hip-hop, pop, house, throwbacks — usually for weddings, bars and parties. Serato is the usual tool. Gigs and business
PA
Public address system: the amplifiers and speakers that make the room loud. Usually separate from the DJ's monitor. Gear and setup
Peak
The loudest moment in a signal. Peaking in the red clips; peaking in the amber is where you want to be. Mixing technique
Phantom power
48 V sent down a microphone cable to power condenser mics. Harmless to dynamic mics, dangerous to some older gear. Gear and setup
Phrase
A group of bars — usually 8, 16 or 32 — that forms a musical unit. Mixing on phrase boundaries is what makes a blend sound intentional. Mixing technique
Pitch fader
The tempo control. Long faders allow fine correction; short faders make beatmatching harder than it needs to be. Gear and setup
Quantise
Snaps your actions to the beatgrid. Useful for cue points and loops; a liability if the grid is wrong. Software
RCA
The unbalanced connector found on most home and budget DJ gear. Fine for short runs, prone to hum over long ones. Gear and setup
Rekordbox
Pioneer/AlphaTheta's library and performance software. The de-facto standard for preparing USB media for club CDJs. Software
Rider
A document listing what a venue must provide for your set — usually decks, a mixer and monitors. Bigger gigs expect one. Gigs and business
Sample rate
How many times per second audio is measured — 44.1 kHz for most music, 48 kHz for video work. Mismatches cause crackles. Software
Scratch
Moving a record or jog wheel back and forth rhythmically to make a percussive sound. A technique, not a genre. Mixing technique
Serato
The dominant software for open-format, scratch and mobile DJs, with the broadest certified hardware range. Software
Set
A performance, usually 60–120 minutes. Also the prepared collection of music for one. Gigs and business
Sibilance
The harsh 's' sound in vocals. A de-esser or a small high-shelf cut fixes it. Mixing technique
Slip mode
Keeps a track playing underneath a scratch or loop, then returns to where it would have been. Makes tricks survivable. Software
Sound check
Testing levels, monitors and routing before doors open. The single most useful habit in live sound. Gigs and business
Stems
Separated vocals, drums, bass and other parts of a track. Modern software can isolate them live, which makes mashups easy and muddy mixes easier. Software
Stylus
The needle. Diamond-tipped, replaceable, and the part that actually touches your records. Gear and setup
Sync
The software's automatic tempo and phase matching. Reliable on correctly gridded files, useless on unanalysed ones. Software
Timecode
The signal on a DVS control record that tells software where the virtual needle is. Software
Tonearm
The arm holding the cartridge. Counterweight and anti-skate settings decide how well it tracks. Gear and setup
Tracker
A scrolling sequencer used in early electronic music and still used for chiptune. Not a DAW in the modern sense. Production
Trim
See gain. The input level control on each channel. Mixing technique
Tune / key
The musical key of a track. Mixing in compatible keys is harmonic mixing. Mixing technique
Turntablism
Using turntables as instruments — cutting, scratching, beat juggling — rather than just playing records. Mixing technique
Unbalanced
A cable type (like RCA or TS jack) that carries signal on one conductor plus a ground. Fine short, noisy long. Gear and setup
VU meter
A level display. Software meters are peak meters; analogue VU meters show average loudness, which is why they look calmer. Mixing technique
Waveform
The visual representation of a track in software. Colour usually indicates frequency content — bass in one colour, highs in another. Software
XLR
The balanced three-pin connector used on pro microphones and PA systems. The most reliable option for long cable runs. Gear and setup
Zone / booth out
A separate output feeding the DJ's own monitors, with its own volume control, independent of the main PA. Gear and setup

The terms that most often get misused

  • Cue. It means both previewing a track and a marked point in it. When someone says "cue it up", they usually mean both at once.
  • Monitor. A studio monitor is a speaker for mixing; a booth monitor is a speaker for the DJ. Same word, opposite ends of the signal chain.
  • CDJ. Almost nobody plays CDs on one. It is the name of a product family, not a format.
  • Trance gate. Not an effect unit — a technique of chopping a channel with the crossfader or a gate in time with the beat.
  • Latency. Confused with buffering. Buffering is an interruption; latency is a constant delay.
