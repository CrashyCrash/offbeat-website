The best DJ controllers under $300

Five controllers that are actually worth the money, what each one gives up to hit the price, and the honest answer about the one that is worth stretching for.

By Offbeat Editorial TeamReviewed October 202611 min read

Under $300 is the most competitive corner of the controller market, and the most confusing to shop in. Every listing promises "pro features", and most of them mean a lit-up pad grid. What actually separates these controllers is less glamorous: whether the software is included, whether the pitch fader is fine enough to beatmatch on, and whether the thing has an audio interface at all.

We read the manufacturer documentation, checked what each unit unlocks in software, and compared October 2026 street prices. These five are the ones we would put our own money into — plus an honest note on the controller that sits just above the line.

The short version

  • Best overall: Hercules DJControl Inpulse 500 — a built-in audio interface, an aux input and Serato or DJUCED in the box. Street price usually lands just under $300.
  • Best for rekordbox and phones: Pioneer DDJ-FLX2 — the cheapest route into the ecosystem most clubs use.
  • Best for learning to beatmatch: Hercules Inpulse 200 — its guide lights show you which way to move the jog wheel before your ears can tell.
  • Best cheap party deck: Numark Party Mix II — light-up pads, Serato DJ Lite included, about $129.
  • Worth stretching for: Pioneer DDJ-FLX4 at about $329. If you can wait a month, it is the better long-term buy.

The picks, in order

Hercules DJControl Inpulse 500 Best overall under $300

The Inpulse 500 is the only controller in this bracket that behaves like a piece of audio equipment rather than a toy. It has a real built-in audio interface, so the sound leaves the controller rather than the laptop's headphone jack, plus an aux input for a phone or a second source — genuinely useful at a party where someone wants to play a track off their device.

You get eight performance pads per deck, a filter per channel and a layout that maps cleanly onto Serato DJ Lite or Hercules' own DJUCED. The pitch faders are long enough to make small corrections, which is the difference between learning to beatmatch and learning to fight your gear.

  • What it does well: built-in interface, aux input, solid jog feel, two software options in the box.
  • What you give up: no balanced outputs, and the club-standard workflow is rekordbox, which this does not run.
  • Who should buy it: anyone who wants the most capable two-channel deck for the money and is not set on rekordbox.

Pioneer DDJ-FLX2 Best for rekordbox and mobile setups

The FLX2 is small, bus-powered and happy with a phone or tablet as well as a laptop. That matters more than it sounds: rekordbox is the software most clubs run, so starting here means the library you build now is the library you take to a booth later. It also unlocks djay Pro, which makes it the cheapest streaming-first setup we would recommend.

The trade-off is physical. It is a compact unit with small jogs and fewer controls than the Inpulse, and it feels like what it is: an entry point rather than a long-term instrument.

  • What it does well: cheapest rekordbox route, phone and tablet support, genuinely portable.
  • What you give up: jog size, control density, and the feel of a bigger deck.
  • Who should buy it: club-focused beginners and anyone who wants to mix from an iPad or phone.

Read the full DDJ-FLX2 review for the controls in detail.

Hercules Inpulse 200 Best for learning to beatmatch

The Inpulse 200's party trick is its Beatmatch Guide: lights on the deck tell you whether the incoming track is too fast or too slow, and which way to move the wheel. Used properly — look at the lights for the first week, then cover them — it shortens the frustrating stage where you can hear that something is wrong but not what.

It is a smaller, lighter deck than the Inpulse 500 and runs DJUCED rather than Serato. If you already know you want Serato or rekordbox, buy the FLX2 or the 500 instead.

  • What it does well: teaching aids that actually work, low price, simple setup.
  • What you give up: software flexibility and the feel of a larger deck.
  • Who should buy it: complete beginners who want structure while they learn.

Numark Mixtrack Pro FX Best Serato layout on a budget

If you have watched other people DJ and want the layout you have seen, this is the cheapest way to get something close: two channels, long pitch faders, a full pad grid and Serato DJ Lite included. It is an older design than the AlphaTheta units, and it feels it — but the layout teaches transferable habits.

  • What it does well: familiar layout, long pitch faders, Serato included.
  • What you give up: newer build quality and a couple of convenience features found on the FLX2.
  • Who should buy it: Serato-focused beginners who want a full-size layout without spending $400+.

Numark Party Mix II Best for parties and the smallest budget

About $129, Serato DJ Lite included, and a light show built into the pads. It is not a serious practice instrument — the controls are small and the pitch faders are short — but for a house party, a first taste of mixing, or a second deck to leave at a friend's place, it does the job and it is genuinely fun.

  • What it does well: price, portability, built-in lights, software included.
  • What you give up: precision. This is the wrong deck to learn manual beatmatching on.
  • Who should buy it: casual users and party hosts, not people planning to play out.

The one worth stretching for

The Pioneer DDJ-FLX4 sits at about $329 — roughly $30–80 above this bracket depending on the week. It has full-size jogs, runs both rekordbox and Serato, and has the layout you will meet in a club booth. If you can wait a month and save the difference, it is the better buy, and it is the controller we recommend in the beginner guide for exactly that reason.

If $300 is a hard ceiling, the Inpulse 500 is the right answer and you will not regret it. The gap between them is feel and ecosystem, not capability.

Five checks before you click buy

  1. Is the software a full licence or a Lite version? Serato DJ Lite and rekordbox's free tier are usable, but they cap features. The listing usually says which one you get.
  2. Does it have an audio interface? If the sound comes out of your laptop's headphone jack, you have bought a very expensive mouse.
  3. Are the pitch faders long enough? Short faders make fine tempo corrections almost impossible, which is the main thing you are practising.
  4. What outputs does it have? RCA is fine at home; you need a plan for XLR or TRS when you play out.
  5. Has it been discontinued? A used bargain on a discontinued model can be excellent value — or a dead end for software support. Check the manufacturer's page before buying.

Frequently asked questions

Is a $300 controller good enough to learn on?

Yes. Everything here is capable of teaching phrasing, EQing and manual beatmatching. What you are missing is build quality, output options and jog size — none of which stops you learning.

Can I play a real gig with one?

For a house party or a small bar, yes, with the right cables. For a club, the limiting factor is usually outputs and the fact that the booth expects CDJs or a club-standard layout.

Should I buy used to get more controller for the money?

Often, yes — a used mid-range deck beats a new budget one if you can check it. Look for sticky jog wheels, dead pads and a missing software licence. Our budget guide covers what to inspect.

Do I need a laptop as well?

For every controller on this page except the ones with phone support, yes — and a laptop that meets your software's minimum spec. Budget for it before you budget for a bigger deck.
