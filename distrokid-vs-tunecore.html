DistroKid vs TuneCore: the same $24.99, and two different catches

In 2026 the two best-known distributors converged on almost identical pricing, which means the decision is no longer about the sticker price. It is about scheduling, the cut taken on social platforms, and whether your catalogue survives a cancelled subscription.

By Offbeat Editorial TeamReviewed October 20269 min read
Compare the real costHow we research

DistroKid and TuneCore used to be an easy argument: DistroKid was the cheap unlimited one, TuneCore was the per-release one with a grown-up dashboard. In 2026 both sell a $24.99 unlimited plan, both sell a $44.99 one above it, and both promise 100% of streaming royalties. So the honest comparison is no longer "which is cheaper". It is "which of these two nearly identical contracts do you want to be inside for the next three years".

We read both pricing pages, both sets of add-ons and the takedown terms in October 2026. Here is what changes the maths, with the numbers shown.

The four differences that decide it

1. Release scheduling on the cheapest plan

This is the one most artists miss, and it is not a small thing. DistroKid's $24.99 Musician plan does not include custom release dates — you cannot pick a Friday three weeks out and set a pre-save window, because scheduling sits on Musician Plus at $44.99. TuneCore's $24.99 Rising Artist plan includes release scheduling. If you are the kind of artist who plans a campaign around a date, the comparison at $24.99 is not a tie: it is $44.99 versus $24.99.

2. What happens on social platforms

Neither service takes a slice of your Spotify royalties on a paid plan. Both take a slice of a different pot: money generated on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, where your recording is used in someone else's video.

  • DistroKid sells YouTube Content ID as an add-on (roughly $4.95 per single or $14.95 per album per year) and keeps about 20% of the ad revenue it collects.
  • TuneCore includes Content ID on its unlimited plans but applies a similar 20% cut to social and user-generated-platform earnings.

In other words, the "we take 0%" line on both homepages is true for streaming and not true for social. If a track is likely to be used in clips, budget for the 20% either way and treat the add-on fee as the tiebreaker.

3. What happens when you stop paying

Subscription distribution means the catalogue is rented. Stop the subscription and the releases come down unless you have paid for permanence:

  • DistroKid's Leave a Legacy is a one-off fee per release — about $29 for a single, $49 for an album — paid on top of the subscription, and it applies to each release separately. Ten singles is roughly $290 to make your catalogue permanent.
  • TuneCore's per-release option works differently: you pay for a term, renew it, and the release stays up while the term is active. There is no separate permanence fee, but there is also no "pay once, live forever" unless you keep renewing.

Either way, a DJ with a growing catalogue should think about the exit cost before choosing the cheaper entry plan. This is the sort of clause people discover when they take a break from releasing for a year.

4. Add-ons and the small print

DistroKid's add-on list is long and priced per release: Store Maximizer for new stores, a discovery pack for Shazam and Siri registration, cover-song licensing at about $12 per cover per year, Dolby Atmos delivery as a one-off. TuneCore bundles more of this into its tiers and sells publishing administration as a service that takes a commission on what it collects. Bundled beats cheap only if you would have bought the add-ons anyway.

What a DJ actually needs from a distributor

This site is written for DJs and producers, so a word about the release you are probably planning. Three things are true regardless of which distributor you pick:

  • Mixes are a licensing problem, not a distribution problem. A recorded set full of other people's records cannot be distributed to Spotify under either service. Put mixes on Mixcloud, SoundCloud or your own site, and read how to promote a mix for the workflow that does work.
  • Edits and bootlegs get removed. A white-label edit is a derivative work. Distributing it is how artists lose an account, and both services enforce this.
  • Your own productions are the catalogue. A DJ with three original tracks on streaming and a Bandcamp page has something a promoter can check. That is the reason to distribute at all — not the royalties, which at small scale buy a coffee.

If your release is a DJ-friendly edit or a sample-flipping beat, read where to license samples before you upload anything.

Verdict

Choose DistroKid if

You release constantly and plan little

Uploads are unlimited on every tier, the flow is fast, and the flat fee rewards volume. Accept that custom release dates need the $44.99 plan, that Content ID costs extra, and that permanence is a fee per release. If you are a producer dropping a track a month with no campaign around it, this is still the least friction.

Choose TuneCore if

You want scheduling and a grown-up dashboard

The $24.99 plan includes release dates, Content ID and revenue splits — the things a DJ actually needs to line a release up with a gig. The $54.99 Professional tier is the cheapest custom-label option of the two, and the per-release model suits artists who release twice a year.

If you are not sure, run a year on whichever is cheaper for your release count, then move before your catalogue gets large. Moving later is possible — distributors all support it — but the takedown-and-reupload of a bigger catalogue is a weekend you will not enjoy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are DistroKid and TuneCore the same price in 2026?

At the entry level, yes. DistroKid's Musician plan and TuneCore's Rising Artist plan both cost $24.99 a year and both include unlimited uploads to 150+ stores. Their mid tiers both cost $44.99. The differences now sit in what each plan includes and in the add-ons that follow.

Which one should a DJ or producer choose for a first release?

If you plan to release on a schedule — Friday drops, pre-saves, a release date you choose — TuneCore's $24.99 plan includes that scheduling and DistroKid's does not. If you upload constantly and rarely plan a campaign, DistroKid's unlimited model is simpler.

Do either of them take a cut of my royalties?

Neither takes a cut of streaming royalties from Spotify, Apple Music and the other DSPs on a paid plan — you pay through the subscription instead. Both take a share of money earned on social and user-generated platforms such as YouTube Content ID, TikTok and Instagram: DistroKid's Content ID add-on costs extra and keeps 20% of the ad revenue it collects, and TuneCore applies a similar 20% to social-platform earnings.

What happens to my music if I stop paying?

On a subscription plan, releases generally come down. That is the part artists discover late. DistroKid sells Leave a Legacy per release ($29 for a single, $49 for an album) to keep a track live permanently, and the fee applies per release, so it compounds with catalogue size. Before committing a year's worth of releases to any distributor, read the takedown clause for the plan you are on.

Can I distribute DJ mixes, edits or bootlegs?

Not the ones that use other people's recordings without clearance. A DJ mix of licensed tracks needs every rightsholder's permission, and a bootleg or edit is a derivative work. Distributors reject these or remove them after a claim, and repeated claims can close the account. Release your own productions, official remixes you were commissioned to make, and mixes only where you hold the rights.

Is there a cheaper option than either?

Yes, if you release rarely: one-time-fee distributors such as CD Baby, or free services such as Amuse and RouteNote, charge nothing upfront and take a percentage, or charge per release instead of per year. That maths wins for a two-single catalogue and loses for a monthly release schedule.
